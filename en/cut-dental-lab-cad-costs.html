How to cut your lab's CAD costs without losing quality

The cost of a design isn't the $4 unit — it's the software seat, the salary, the training and the idle time behind it. Here are five ways to lower what design really costs you, without cutting corners on the work.

By Alejandro Carvajal · Dental CAD/CAM designer 6 min read Updated Nov 2026

When a lab adds up the cost of design, it usually looks at the time a technician spends on a case. But the real number is bigger and mostly hidden: a CAD seat, annual maintenance or subscription, training, and — the quiet killer — the hours a salaried designer is paid while the queue is empty. Those are fixed costs: you pay them whether ten cases come in this week or none.

Cutting CAD cost isn't about paying your designer less. It's about turning that fixed cost into a variable one, and about not paying twice for the same unit through remakes. Five levers do most of the work.

1. Turn fixed cost into variable

This is the big one. A salaried in-house designer and a software seat cost the same in a slow month as a busy one. If your volume is uneven — and for most labs it is — you're paying for capacity you don't always use.

Outsourcing design per case flips that: you pay only when a case actually comes in. Overflow months cost more, quiet months cost nothing. For a lab whose workload swings, that alone can be the difference between a profitable quarter and a thin one.

2. Don't buy a seat you can't fill

A perpetual CAD license plus modules, or an annual subscription, only pays for itself above a certain weekly volume. Below that line, the per-unit cost of owning the software is brutal.

  • If you design only a handful of units a week, outsourcing is almost always cheaper than a seat.
  • If you're ramping up, outsource now and buy the seat later, once volume justifies it — not before.

3. Kill remakes before they happen

A remake is the most expensive unit in the lab: you pay for it twice, plus the shipping, plus the unhappy client. Most remakes trace back to the design — a margin that wasn't verified, a contact that was too tight, an occlusion left to chance.

One avoided remake pays for several designs. Human-verified design — margins, contacts and occlusion checked by hand, with a preview you approve — costs a little more per unit and saves far more in remakes and chair time.

4. Cut the back-and-forth

Hidden cost lives in the messages: unclear prescriptions, missing scan-body files, shade notes that arrive after the design is done. Every round trip is wasted time on both sides.

  • Send the scan, the prescription and the library in one go.
  • Use a case code instead of patient data — faster and safer.
  • Work with a designer who confirms viability up front instead of halfway through.

5. Keep the margin where it's made

Cutting cost shouldn't mean giving away your business. The margin is in the fabrication and the client relationship — so keep those. Outsource only the design, mill in-house, and deliver under your own name. You lower the cost of the one step that's easy to hand off while keeping everything that makes you money.

When in-house still wins

To be fair: if you run high, steady volume — enough to keep a designer busy every day — an in-house seat can be the cheapest option per unit, and the control is worth it. The math turns on utilization. The lower and bumpier your volume, the more outsourcing saves. The higher and steadier it is, the more in-house makes sense.
