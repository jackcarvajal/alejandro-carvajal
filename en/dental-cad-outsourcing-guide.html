Guide for labs & clinics

How to outsource dental CAD design without losing control

Outsourcing your digital design frees your bench and your CAM machines without adding software seats or payroll. Here is how the workflow actually works, what to send, what it costs, and how to vet a remote partner before you trust them with a case.

By Alejandro Carvajal · Dental CAD/CAM designer 6 min read Updated Nov 2026

More labs and clinics outsource their CAD design than ever — not because they can't design, but because design is the bottleneck, not the milling. A single technician can only draw so many units a day, software seats and training are expensive, and demand is rarely steady. Outsourcing the design lets you keep the fabrication, the margin and the client relationship while someone else absorbs the variable workload.

This guide is written for the person deciding whether to hand a case to a remote designer for the first time. It covers what you can realistically outsource, how the hand-off works, and — most importantly — how to protect your cases and your clients while you do it.

What you can outsource

Almost any design that starts from a scan or a CBCT can be done remotely and returned as an STL you mill or print in-house:

  • Crowns & bridges — anatomic or reduced copings, press and monolithic.
  • Veneers & smile design — facially-driven DSD, layered or monolithic morphology.
  • Implant esthetics — screw-retained crowns, custom abutments, Ti-base and emergence-profile shaping.
  • Full-arch / All-on-X — hybrid frameworks, titanium bars, immediate provisionals.
  • Clear-aligner planning — staging, attachments and IPR, STL per stage for in-house printing.
  • Surgical guides — fully-guided planning from CBCT + intraoral scan, ready to print.

What you keep is everything that touches your client and your equipment: the prescription, the milling, the finishing and the delivery. The designer never sees your patient and never contacts your client.

How the workflow works

A good remote design hand-off has only three moving parts, and none of them should involve shipping a physical model:

  1. You send the files. An intraoral scan or CBCT, your prescription and any shade or library notes.
  2. The case is designed and you approve it. You receive a preview (screenshots or a 3D file), request changes until it matches your prescription, and only then is the final file released.
  3. You mill or print in-house. You import the verified STL and fabricate on your side — same day, same turnaround, under your own name.

The approval step is what keeps you in control: nothing is “final” until you say so, and revisions should be part of the price, not an upsell.

Which files to send

You don't need to change how you scan. A remote designer should accept the formats your scanner and planning software already export:

  • Scans: STL, PLY or OBJ — or the native Exocad .dentalProject / 3Shape Communicate case.
  • Implant cases: the scan plus the scan-body file and the implant system/library you use.
  • Surgical guides: the intraoral scan plus a CBCT in DICOM format.

Files move over WhatsApp, WeTransfer, Dropbox or Google Drive — whatever you already use. There is no portal to learn and no software to install on your side.

Turnaround & cost

Per-unit design is inexpensive compared with a domestic design seat plus a salaried technician. Pricing is usually quoted per unit, in USD, with no contract and no minimum order, so you can test a single case before you commit any volume. Simple design cases are ready within a maximum of 24 hours; full-arch and guides take a little longer because they carry more planning.

Watch the real math. The point of outsourcing is not just a cheaper unit — it's converting a fixed cost (software + salary + training) into a variable one you only pay when you have the work. That's what lets a small lab take on overflow without hiring.

Quality, confidentiality & white-label

This is the part that actually decides whether you'll outsource again. Three things matter more than price:

  • Human-verified design. Auto-generated proposals look fine on screen and fail at the margin. A designer should check contacts, occlusion and emergence by hand before release.
  • Confidentiality. Your files should be used only to design your case and nothing else. Send a case code instead of patient identifiers wherever you can.
  • White-label by default. A remote designer works behind your lab's name. Your clients never see them, and they never approach your clients. If a partner can't promise that in writing, keep looking.

How to vet a design partner

Before you send a real case, a short checklist saves a lot of trouble:

  • Do they design in the software you trust (Exocad, 3Shape)?
  • Are revisions included until the design matches your prescription?
  • Is the STL exported for an open CAM system compatible with your mill?
  • Do they put confidentiality and white-label in writing?
  • Will they take a single trial case — no contract — so you can judge the result yourself?

If the answer to all five is yes, a trial case costs you almost nothing and tells you everything.
